Two simulated drones meet, and each signs the same encounter record

simulation: no flight happenedtoday: for the record, afterwardsabout 10 years: shared airspace (a forecast, not a fact) Two quadcopters from two operators cross. Within … m they talk directly, over a simulated link that loses and delays messages. Each signs "I see you" (its key id, its position and altitude as it believes them, a fresh number from the other), then both sign the same record of the pair. Each keeps it in its own notebook. This is evidence afterwards. It is not collision avoidance and not a safety system.

What this does not show

  • That either position was true: the check judges what each aircraft signed as its believed position, not whether it was correct.
  • That the aircraft were near each other: the check judges whether the two believed positions are within 100 m of each other (the 80 m link range plus 20 m); it does not judge the distance between the aircraft.
  • Which aircraft: the check judges which keys signed; it does not judge who held a key.
  • An encounter absent from both copies: the check judges encounters that at least one copy holds.
  • With one log, the check judges that log's records and signatures; whether an encounter was removed from it is judged only against the other aircraft's copy (case "one log only").
  • When it happened: each message's time is the aircraft's own clock, signed by that aircraft; nothing here is checked against another clock.
  • Anything in real time. The record exists after the exchange; nothing here steers, warns or separates aircraft.
  • That an exchange cut short is kept: if the link drops before both signatures arrive, nothing is recorded in this demo.
  • That the message format is a standard: it is a demo format (kind tags tet_demo_encounter_seen_v1, tet_demo_encounter_v1), not a protocol anyone has agreed to.

What exists, compared (from memory, unchecked)

Remote identificationEach drone broadcasts its id and position for anyone near to receive. One-way, in real time; as far as I know it is not signed in the common schemes, and I know of no scheme that keeps a two-party record of it.
ADS-BCrewed aircraft broadcast position from their own navigation; receivers on the ground and in the air hear it. Not authenticated in its common form (from memory, unchecked).
Detect and avoidOn-board sensing and logic that keeps aircraft apart, in real time. That is the safety function; this record is not one and does not replace it.
Flight logsEach aircraft keeps its own log, held by its operator. No other party's signature is in it.
This recordNot real time. After the fact, each operator's copy carries the other's signature on the same record, so an edit or a removal in one copy is shown against the other.

Why not a chain between the drones

A chain makes many parties agree on one order of events. Two aircraft that meet are two parties, and they only need to agree on one record: two signatures over the same bytes do that, in three short messages each (a fresh number, an "I see you", a signature), over a link that loses and delays them. Consensus between aircraft would need more messages, more rounds and more time in range, and a third party to break a tie; none of that makes this record harder to forge. So: peer to peer in the air, yes; a chain in the air, no.

What a stamp on the ground would add

Later, on the ground, either operator could publish the record's hash to a chain (the notebook's publish, not run here). That would add one thing: the record existed before block N. A stamp would show that the record existed before block N. Not judged here: when the record existed. It would let either side show that its copy was fixed early. It would not show that a position was true, and it is not needed for the check on this page. Not done in this demo.
